## Hypothesis: The Emergence of Life from a Partially Replicating RNA Network

#### 1. Agreement with the RNA World Hypothesis, with Modification
While I generally agree with the RNA world hypothesis, I propose that fully self-replicating RNA molecules did not emerge all at once. Instead, naturally synthesized RNAs likely exhibited only partial replication abilities. These imperfect replication events, along with subsequent molecular transformations and fragmentation, formed a dynamic system whose cumulative behavior laid the foundation for the origin of life.

#### 2. Prebiotic Network Dynamics Without Natural Selection
In the absence of predators and competitive pressures, RNAs and their derivatives likely replicated and interacted meaninglessly at first. However, due to RNA's inherent instability, structurally weak or functionless molecules would degrade over time. In contrast, relatively stable and functionally meaningful variants would persist and interact more frequently, forming a kind of emergent chemical selection.

#### 3. Formation of a Proto-Cellular "RNA Soup"
This system of interacting RNAs created what could be called a proto-metabolic soup—a dynamic network of molecules that:
· Supported slow and partial replication processes,  
· Exchanged information with the external environment, and  
· Maintained a degree of internal homeostasis.
As such systems accumulated and stabilized, the size and complexity of the "soup" would have gradually increased.

#### 4. Three Proto-Life Functions from Emergent Properties
The expanding RNA soup exhibited three proto-biological capacities:
· Partial inheritance via replication (primitive genetics),
· External responsiveness through interaction (reaction), and
· Internal stabilization (homeostasis).
These properties correspond to the foundational functions seen in modern life.

#### 5. Internal Divergence Within the Expanding Soup
As the soup expanded, it likely developed a heterogeneous structure—continually mixing, splitting, and recombining. While remaining broadly connected as a system, localized regions may have differed slightly in molecular composition or interaction patterns. While maintaining local stability and interacting with the broader molecular network, some of these regions may have benefited from partial insulation from their surroundings, yet continued to operate within the soup.

#### 6. Emergence of Localized Units: Proto-Cells
Eventually, some molecular collectives within the soup may have found it advantageous to remain locally grouped, rather than continuing to mix freely with the broader environment. These clusters behaved increasingly like primitive cells, beginning to act as semi-autonomous units. Yet, because they originated from the soup, they retained a drive toward growth and interaction with surrounding resources.

#### 7. Structural Advantages in Growth and Re-formation
As proto-cells grew, they often reached unstable sizes and split—beginning a primitive form of reproduction. Groups with more cohesive structures or layouts that naturally supported division had a clear advantage. When collapse occurred, such organized collectives could reassemble more easily, re-establishing internal structure and boundaries faster than randomly mixed ones. Even without full heredity, this structural resilience offered a selective benefit in the unstable prebiotic environment.

#### 8. Emergence of Division Guidance and Proto-Genetic Selection
Among these resilient collectives, some began to exhibit internal patterns that subtly influenced how they divided—even if only loosely or by chance. In environments where failed divisions returned collectives to the molecular soup, any structure that biased reproduction could have improved their stability—even without full heredity. These early cues may have arisen spontaneously—not as deliberate blueprints, but still as usable signals. They may have been an early precursor to heritable control, subtly influencing the path toward genetic mechanisms.

#### 9. Primitive Genetic Information as a Stability Mechanism
Similar to how modern nuclei help maintain cellular homeostasis, early informational molecules—though not fully self-replicating—may have played a role in stabilizing these proto-cells. Their existence did not require high-fidelity replication; rather, they acted as flexible, error-prone regulators of internal structure and division patterns.

#### 10. Necessity of Boundary and Information Isolation
As such proto-cells developed more sophisticated internal regulation (e.g., primitive division instructions or metabolic stability), the need to isolate key informational components from external mixing increased. Selective compartmentalization—perhaps through rudimentary membrane-like boundaries—would have helped protect these fragile instructions from degradation. Over time, this internal segregation may have laid the conceptual groundwork for the emergence of centralized control structures, such as the nucleus in modern cells.

#### 11. Toward Functional Specialization: Feeding, Growth, and Division
Even without complete replication, these units began displaying three hallmark life behaviors:
· Consuming the surrounding RNA soup (proto-feeding),
· Accreting size and complexity (proto-growth), and
· Dividing with rough informational continuity (proto-inheritance).
These behaviors suggest the early presence of meaningful functional specialization within the network.

#### 12. Threshold Toward True Cellular Behavior
At this stage, proto-cells possessed most features we associate with life: internal organization, responsiveness, primitive metabolism, and imperfect but directional inheritance. Over time, repeated cycles of growth, division, and refinement could lead to increasingly accurate internal replication and coordination—eventually producing entities we might classify as true cells with primitive genetic systems.

#### 13. Implication for Modern Cellular Systems
If modern cells still contain RNA-based regulatory systems—or other small and large molecules that could plausibly have arisen under primitive chemical conditions—this may suggest that such components are evolutionary remnants of an earlier, chemically rich and semi-replicative molecular network. In this sense, the theory may offer a plausible explanatory framework for the origin of life.
